# Pide Directo POS

### A clearer order flow for restaurant staff. Designed and built in React in two weeks.

<Summary

## Users and context

The POS is an internal tool for restaurant staff: cashiers, waiters and managers. They take orders, apply modifiers and charge customers.

It runs on phones, tablets and desktops. Most restaurants use a dedicated tablet for it.

The old interface had layout problems: the main action on a screen wasn't always the most visible thing, categories took as much space as products, and heavy lines split every section.

## The flow

An order goes through four steps. Two got a new layout; the other two got the new styles.

### 1. Order type

The old screen had a giant box on the right that only said "Select the order type", with the actual order types off to the side. The layout didn't prioritize the one action that mattered: choosing the type.

The redesign makes choosing the order type the main action on the screen.

#### After

### 2. Menu

Categories took up the left side of the screen and were as big as products, or bigger. That's the wrong hierarchy: products are what staff add to the order.

The redesign moves categories above the products and makes them smaller. Products get the space and become the most important element on the screen.

#### Before
![Menu, before](/case-studies/pos/02-menu-before.webp)

#### After
![Menu, after](/case-studies/pos/02-menu-after.webp)

### 3. Modifiers

Here the change was mostly visual, to make the process less confusing. Each group of options has a clear title and its rule ("required", "max 1"). Options are cards with their price. What's been chosen collects in a summary panel on the right, with the button to add it to the order.

#### Before
![Modifiers, before](/case-studies/pos/03-modifiers-before.webp)

#### After
![Modifiers, after](/case-studies/pos/03-modifiers-after.webp)

### 4. Payment

Payment kept its structure: payment method, contact details, a keypad, and the total with the amount still missing. The work was making it consistent with the rest of the interface. Payment methods became buttons with icons, and the amount and the total are easier to read at a glance.

#### After

### Across the flow

Heavy lines used to split every section. The redesign groups related content in boxes instead, so a dense screen reads as a few clear blocks.

## Phone, tablet and desktop

The old POS wasn't responsive. I designed and built it for every screen staff use: the dedicated tablet most restaurants have, desktop, and phone. The phone version feels native, with cards anchored to the bottom of the screen to make navigation easier.

Waiters used it the most on phones, to take orders from their own phone in the restaurant.

![Mobile views](/case-studies/pos/05-phone.webp)

## Constraints as decisions

The project was planned to fit two weeks of design and development, and it shipped complete.

The goal wasn't a full redesign, because it had to ship fast. Order type and menu got new layouts, because their hierarchy was wrong. Modifiers and payment kept their structure and got the new styles. The whole flow now looks consistent.

## Building it

I built it in React with plain CSS. Its backend also powers the [Pide Directo web menu](/projects/web-menu-redesign), the customer-facing side of the same system.

## What changed

It shipped to every business using Pide Directo. Feedback from the internal team was very positive: they praised the new look and the usability. There was no formal testing with restaurant staff.

## Version 2: checkout

**My role:** I designed it alone and had help implementing the frontend. It shipped.

Version 1 fixed the order flow. Charging needed more options: restaurants needed to save an order without charging it, apply credits and discounts, add tips, and take more payment methods. They also needed to see which orders were still open.

### Active orders on the main screen

The main screen now shows the orders that are still open, next to the order types. Each card shows the customer, the ticket, the table and the time since the order was placed, with the next action: assign a delivery driver, edit, or print the bill. The main screen started to look like an app.

![Main screen with order types and pending orders](/case-studies/pos/06-v2-orders.webp)

### Editing an order

The order stays visible on the right while staff add products, with the subtotal and total, and two ways out: charge now or save without charging.

![Editing an order](/case-studies/pos/07-v2-edit-order.webp)

### A flexible checkout

Paying became its own flow: payment method, amount, customer details, and a summary.

- **More payment methods**: cash, card, US dollars, AMEX and other.
- **Registered payments**: the amount still missing and the payments already registered stay visible at the bottom of every step.
- **Everything checkout needs**: charge, save without charging, add a tip, add a discount, apply promotions or credits, and complete with an invoice.
- **Summary always in view**: on tablet, the total, change and amount missing sit on the right while staff work through the steps.

![Paying an order on tablet](/case-studies/pos/08-v2-pay-order.webp)

On the phone, the same flow runs one step per screen.

![Checkout on the phone: payment method, amount, customer details and summary](/case-studies/pos/09-v2-phone-checkout.webp)

## What I'd measure

- **Time per order**: from opening a new order to payment, at peak hours.
- **Taps per order**: how many steps the most common orders take.
- **Errors**: orders saved with the wrong type, missing or wrong modifiers, and voids.
- **Learning time**: how long a new staff member takes to process orders without help.
- **Checkout**: time from opening the payment to a closed order, and how often orders are saved without charging, split across payments, or get tips, discounts or credits.
